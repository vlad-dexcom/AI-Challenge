# Barbell Back Squat Technique

## What good squat technique is trying to achieve

The barbell back squat is a coordinated lower-body strength lift, not just a leg exercise. A strong rep keeps the bar balanced over the middle of the foot while the hips, knees, trunk, and upper back share the work. When technique is sound, the descent looks controlled, the bottom position is owned rather than collapsed into, and the ascent is forceful without turning into a good morning.

There is no single perfect squat shape. A lifter with long femurs may lean forward more than a short-torsoed lifter and still be squatting well. The point is not to copy one silhouette. The point is to use a stance, bar position, and depth that let you stay braced and move the bar efficiently.

## Rack setup and bar position

Set the rack so you can unrack by standing up, not by tiptoeing or squatting the bar out. A poor rack height wastes energy before the set even starts.

Most back squats use either a high-bar or low-bar position.

### High-bar

The bar rests on the upper traps. This usually allows a more upright torso and more knee travel. Many lifters prefer it for quad emphasis, deep squatting, and general athletic lifting.

### Low-bar

The bar sits lower across the rear delts. This often allows more load, but it also requires a firmer upper-back shelf and usually a bit more torso lean.

In either style, the hands help create tension rather than hold the weight. Pull the shoulder blades together enough to make a stable shelf. If the wrists are bent sharply or the elbows flare too far back, the upper back often goes soft.

## Stance, feet, and balance

A useful starting stance is around shoulder width, sometimes a little wider, with the toes turned out modestly. From there, adjust based on comfort, depth, and hip structure. Your best stance is the one that lets the knees track naturally, the hips clear space, and the whole foot stay connected to the floor.

Think tripod foot contact:

- base of the big toe
- base of the little toe
- heel

If you lose one point of contact, balance usually shifts. Rolling to the toes often makes the bar drift forward. Rolling to the outer foot can make the knees cave or the hips sway.

## Bracing and breathing

Bracing gives the squat its structure. Before each rep, inhale into the abdomen and lower ribcage, then stiffen the trunk as if preparing to absorb impact. The goal is expansion around the entire midsection, not a chest-only breath.

For heavier work, keep that brace through most of the rep and reset at the top if needed. A belt can improve feedback and help you create more pressure, but it is not a substitute for learning to brace.

## The descent

Start the squat by unlocking the knees and hips together. If the hips shoot back first, the lift becomes too hinge-dominant. If the knees dive forward without enough hip bend, the bottom may feel cramped. A simple cue is to sit down and between the hips while keeping the bar over midfoot.

Let the knees move in line with the toes. They do not need to be jammed dramatically outward, but they should not collapse inward. Descend with enough control to stay balanced, not so slowly that the set becomes a long isometric.

## Depth and bottom position

For many lifters, a productive back squat reaches at least parallel, and often slightly below. In powerlifting, depth is usually judged by the hip crease dropping below the top of the knee. In general training, the best depth is the deepest range you can control without losing spinal position, heel contact, or bar balance.

If the pelvis tucks aggressively under, the heels lift, or the bar shoots forward, pushing deeper is not automatically better. Often the answer is not “try harder,” but rather improve bracing, adjust stance width, point the toes out a bit more, or use a small heel lift.

## Driving out of the hole

The ascent should feel like the chest and hips rise together. A common miss happens when the hips shoot up faster than the shoulders, leaving the lifter folded over. Out of the bottom, think about pushing the floor away while keeping the upper back proud and the midfoot loaded.

Helpful cues include:

- drive the floor down
- keep the chest organized
- let the knees keep contributing
- stand tall without overextending at lockout

A clean finish is simply full knee and hip extension with the bar still stacked over the foot.

## Common faults and likely fixes

### Knees caving inward

This often shows up when the load is too heavy, the feet are unstable, or the hips lose control. Reduce the load enough to keep the knees tracking over the toes and use paused or tempo squats to improve awareness.

### Heels coming up

This usually points to balance drifting forward, limited ankle mobility, or both. Check whether the bar is staying over midfoot. Weightlifting shoes or heel wedges can help some lifters maintain posture and depth.

### Hips rising too quickly

If the squat turns into a hinge, the quads may not be contributing enough or the lifter may be losing brace at the bottom. High-bar squats, front squats, and paused squats are useful correctives.

### Loss of position at depth

A dramatic butt wink or collapse at the bottom can reflect stance mismatch, poor bracing, or mobility limits. Slightly changing stance width or toe angle often helps more than forcing deeper reps.

### Bar moving around on the back

If the bar rolls, rebuild the shelf. Bring the hands in if mobility allows, tighten the upper back, and make sure the bar sits on muscle, not on a slippery or relaxed position.

## Squat variations and when to use them

### Front squat

Front squats demand a more upright torso and expose weak bracing quickly. They are excellent for quad strength and positional awareness.

### Paused squat

A pause of one to three seconds at the bottom removes rebound and teaches control in the hardest position.

### Tempo squat

Slower eccentrics can improve balance, confidence, and discipline without requiring maximal loading.

### Box squat

A box can teach consistent depth and control if used as a target. If you crash onto it or rock backward, the drill stops resembling a normal squat.

### Heel-elevated squat

A small heel lift can help lifters who struggle to reach depth or keep the torso organized because of ankle limitations.

## Warm-up and practice advice

Most squat warm-ups do not need to be elaborate. General movement, a few bodyweight or goblet squats, then progressive barbell sets are often enough. If you need mobility work, choose one or two drills that clearly improve your setup instead of doing a long circuit out of habit.

Technique is often built best with submaximal loads. Sets of three to six reps give repeated chances to practice bracing, bar path, and timing without every set becoming a grind.

## Practical priorities

If you only remember a few squat ideas, keep these:

- create a solid shelf for the bar
- brace before every rep
- stay balanced over the whole foot
- descend with knees and hips working together
- reach usable depth without losing shape
- drive up with shoulders and hips rising together

The squat improves through repetition more than through dramatic cues. When setup, depth, and bar path become repeatable, strength usually follows.
