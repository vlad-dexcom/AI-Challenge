# Deadlift Technique

## What makes the deadlift technical

The deadlift starts from a dead stop, so setup quality matters more than most lifters realize. There is very little room to hide a bad position. If the bar begins too far forward, if the hips are in the wrong place, or if tension is missing before the plates leave the floor, the pull immediately becomes harder.

A good deadlift keeps the bar close to the body, uses a clear hip hinge, and coordinates leg drive with hip extension. The movement should look patient at the start and decisive once the bar breaks the floor.

## Conventional deadlift setup

Stand with the feet about hip width apart and place the bar over the middle of the foot. For many lifters that means the bar sits roughly over the knot of the laces when viewed from the side.

Hinge down and take the grip just outside the legs. Use double overhand for warm-ups if possible, then mixed grip or hook grip when needed for heavier sets. Once the hands are set, bring the shins to the bar without knocking it forward, lift the chest enough to flatten the torso, and brace the trunk.

A useful checklist is:

- bar over midfoot
- shins close to the bar
- shoulders slightly in front of it
- hips above squat level but below a stiff-leg position
- abdomen pressurized before the pull begins

## Tension before the bar leaves the floor

Many technical problems come from trying to rip the bar upward. Instead, learn to pull slack out of the system first. Load the bar gradually until you feel the hamstrings tighten, the lats engage, and the body wedge itself between the floor and the bar.

Think about locking the armpits down and making the arms into long straps. The hands connect you to the bar; they do not curl it up. When tension is built well, the start of the pull feels quieter and more powerful.

## The hinge and first pull

The deadlift is a hinge, but it is not a pure back raise. Once braced, push the floor away and let the knees extend enough for the bar to break contact with the floor while the torso angle changes gradually. In a clean first pull, the hips and shoulders rise together.

If the hips shoot up before the bar moves, you probably started too low or too loose. If the bar swings away from the body, you are giving away leverage immediately.

Keep the bar path tight. The bar should track almost vertically and stay very close to the shins and thighs.

## Lockout without overdoing it

As the bar passes the knees, the lift shifts toward stronger hip extension. Finish by squeezing the glutes and standing tall. A strong lockout means the hips and knees are extended while the ribs remain stacked over the pelvis.

There is no need to lean backward at the top. Hyperextending the lower back does not count as extra completion; it usually means the glutes did not finish the job cleanly.

## Lowering the bar

In training, the descent reinforces good mechanics. Hinge at the hips first and let the bar slide down the thighs. After it clears the knees, allow more knee bend so the plates return to the floor in the same path they came up.

A controlled eccentric is especially useful for lifters who are still learning how to stay close to the body.

## Sumo deadlift basics

The sumo deadlift uses a wider stance with the hands gripping inside the legs. The torso is often more upright and the hips start closer to the bar. Some lifters find sumo suits their structure or reduces lower-back strain, but it still depends on good tension and leverage.

For sumo, turn the toes out, let the knees track over them, and think about spreading the floor without rolling onto the outside edges of the feet. Wedge the hips toward the bar rather than squatting straight down to it.

Common sumo priorities:

- bar over midfoot
- chest tall without losing abdominal pressure
- lats tight from the start
- patience off the floor

A frequent mistake is dropping the hips too low. When that happens, the hips rise first and the bar stalls.

## Romanian deadlift technique

The Romanian deadlift begins from the top and emphasizes the hinge rather than a floor pull. Start standing with the bar in your hands, unlock the knees slightly, and push the hips back while the bar stays close to the thighs.

The knees bend a little, but they should not keep drifting forward as you descend. Stop when you reach the deepest position you can control without losing spinal shape or bar proximity. For many people that is around mid-shin.

RDLs are useful for:

- teaching the hinge pattern
- loading the hamstrings at length
- building posterior-chain muscle
- improving deadlift positioning

## Common deadlift faults and fixes

### Bar drifting forward

This increases the load on the lower back and makes lockout harder. Recheck the start position, keep the bar over midfoot, and engage the lats more aggressively.

### Rounded lower back under load

Some upper-back rounding can occur in heavy pulls, but loss of lower-back control is a warning sign. Reduce the load, brace harder, and avoid chasing range of motion you cannot own.

### Hips shooting up

This usually means the start was too squat-like or tension was missing. Raise the hips slightly and learn to pull slack out before committing to the lift.

### Hitching at lockout

If the bar stalls above the knee and climbs in stages, you likely lost position earlier. Paused deadlifts, RDLs, and block pulls can help depending on where the breakdown starts.

### Bent arms

Soft elbows waste force and can irritate the biceps, especially with a mixed grip. Keep the arms long and let the hips and legs drive the movement.

## Helpful deadlift variations

### Paused deadlift

Pausing just off the floor or below the knee teaches bar control and tension.

### Deficit deadlift

A small deficit can improve strength from the floor if you can still set the back and brace properly.

### Block pull or rack pull

These shorten the range and can be useful for specific overload or for training around temporary mobility or fatigue issues.

### Tempo RDL

A slower lowering phase builds awareness of hinge mechanics and hamstring loading.

## Practical cues

Different lifters respond to different cues, but these are commonly useful:

- wedge yourself to the bar
- squeeze the bar to the legs
- push the floor away
- keep the armpits tight
- stand tall, do not lean back

## Final deadlift checklist

Reliable deadlifting usually comes down to a few recurring priorities:

- start with the bar over midfoot
- brace before pulling
- build tension before the plates leave the floor
- keep the bar very close to the body
- let the hips and shoulders rise together early
- finish with the glutes, not a backbend

Whether you pull conventional, sumo, or use the Romanian deadlift as assistance, the principle is the same: respect leverage and make the bar travel the shortest strong path you can control.
